## Abstract
Fossil fuels, land use, and agriculture are the three major sources contributing to the rise in atmospheric greenhouse gases (GHGs). Agricultural activities including the production of rice, animal raising, and aquaculture directly contribute to GHG emissions. Approximately one fourth of all GHG emissions are attributable to agricultural operations primarily as a result of unsustainable farming practices. The GHG emissions from agriculture include carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxide (N2O), methane (CH4). However, measurements of GHGs are laborious, expensive, and technically challenging but are crucial to select the most appropriate and feasible system. Since it is difficult to find a comprehensive overview of the variety of techniques, including latest technologies, in one article, this review aims to provide details of available methodologies, their benefits and drawbacks, with a focus on those that have been extensively tested in various ecosystems and regions. This paper is a purely methodological one which, on purpose, tried to familiarize the reader with the vast range of different developments. Our analysis is not exhaustive and is not intended to be a systematic review. The article summarizes that micrometeorological approaches are suitable for measurement from broader footprints, whereas chamber techniques measuring gases from point sources, are more appropriate for high spatio-temporal resolutions in diverse ecosystems. Stable isotope techniques are the most accurate methods and allow a process-specific quantification of GHGs but require sophisticated equipment.

## Introduction
Measurements of greenhouse gases (GHGs) from agricultural soils, manure, and animal feeding activities are challenging and often unreliable, associated with large uncertainties. Despite that the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) methodology is the most widely used and allows for comparisons [[Citation1](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)], GHG emissions from the same sector differ between countries due to differences in handling, and use of inputs, as well as variations in the calculation and analysis methodology. Techniques that work well for one gas might not work well for a mixture of gases. Accurate measurement and monitoring of gas emissions are greatly hampered by the complexity of environmental factors influencing gas production rates and fluxes. Only a small number of applications support real-time, continuous measurement, necessitating method expansion and new technological advances. Emission evaluations are challenging due to the variety of techniques, the vast range of circumstances in which gases are produced from soil, and the sensitivity of emissions to shifting climatic and soil conditions [[Citation2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Analytical measurement-based quantification of GHGs emissions produce more precise estimates and aid in the evaluation of gas-generating and emission models. For quantifying GHG emissions, cost-effective, portable, selective, and sensitive analytical techniques are lacking. Diverse analyzers, such as gas chromatography (GC), spectroscopic techniques, and newly developed solid-state sensing devices, are used to quantify GHGs concentrations in air samples at trace levels [[Citation3](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. However, these techniques can have disadvantages, such as high installation and maintenance costs. A suitable gas detection and monitoring system would need to be fast, accurate, very sensitive, able to measure continuously, portable, robust, chemically inert, remotely accessible, and inexpensive to purchase and easy to operate [[Citation4](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation5](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Depending on the intended application, measuring techniques must be carefully selected, and assumptions for each approach must be considered in light of each unique measurement circumstance. However, many methods have been conventionally used and while at the same time new techniques have been developed their suitability for diversified ecosystems and purposes with the limited resources have never been compared and summarized.
The available literature focuses on the measurement of a single gas or a single measurement method; they do not show how each analytical methodology can be used to perform the simultaneous determination of one or more gases. Here we provide a systematic review with critical analysis of different detection and sampling techniques ranging from low to high costs for the global researchers for measuring GHG emissions. The objective of this article is to direct the future advances, the pros, and cons of different methods for determining GHG emissions, as well as how well they can be applied to gases of interest. The popular sample and field measurement tools along with the most effective analytical methods for taking GHG measurements are included in this review. Our aim is to provide a comprehensive guidance to select the most suitable measurement technology with their potential and limitations for using in variable fields.
## Measurement of greenhouse gas emissions
It is imperative to comprehend the contributions of both human activities and natural processes on the radiative properties of the atmosphere. Disproportionate emissions are related to certain sources (cattle, poultry, crop lands, fossil fuel burning etc.). Land use contributed about 25% of all land-use emissions [[Citation6](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Estimation of the GHGs flow and the evaluation of effective remedies for reducing emissions are based on field measurements [[Citation7](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. To accurately assess GHGs emissions in agricultural fields and aquatic systems, a variety of measurement strategies, each having strengths and constraints [[Citation8–10](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)] are available. Chamber-based techniques and micrometeorological techniques are the two most widely used methods [[Citation8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation11](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)].
### Chamber methods
The main techniques for measuring GHGs from agricultural areas are chamber-based methods. It is the most popular and widely used method for monitoring gas emissions from soil surfaces using chambers, which allows gases of interest to gather in a defined volume. Chamber methods are recognized as the most widely used to estimate the gas emissions from agricultural and animal feeding activities [[Citation12](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Chamber-based techniques sampled from a constrained above-ground headspace and concentrate on changes in gas concentration at the soil surface. Their high coefficients of variation are caused by the spatial variance in soil gas fluxes however, they allow for many treatments and small-area assessments. Chamber sizes can vary from less than 1 m3 to more than 150 m3. To measure the changes in CO2, N2O, and CH4 concentration, samples are taken at several times throughout chamber closure. The effect of chamber size and deployment time determines the level of accuracy that can be achieved [[Citation13](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)].
There are two different kinds of chambers: steady state (SS), where the gas loop is open and the concentration of the measured gas does not increase over time, and non-steady state (NSS), where the gas loop is closed and the gas concentrations in the chamber increase. The conditions inside the chamber should not be different to the outside conditions (e.g. temperature, pressure) to avoid artefacts [[Citation12](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation14](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Thus appropriate insulation and vents that allow transmission of pressure differences are recommended. To avoid artefacts due to insertion of the chambers into the soil frames should be pre-installed on which the chamber can be placed.
There are three basic chamber-based techniques:
  1. Open soil chambers with flow-through air circulation,
  2. Closed soil chambers with closed-loop air circulation,
  3. Static closed chambers with no air circulation


Open chambers, also referred to as steady-state flow-through chambers, continuously circulate outside air [[Citation15](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The gas concentration in the chamber stabilizes and reaches equilibrium after some time. A gas analyzer is then used to calculate the gas flux from the difference in concentration between the chamber’s inlet and outlet. Open chambers perform better than static closed chambers when low gas concentrations are anticipated to be near the equipment detection limits and when the gases of concern are reactive [[Citation14](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Depending on the source of the airflow through the chamber, open chambers can be either passive or dynamic [[Citation16](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. GHG emissions from agricultural activities have been extensively investigated using passive and dynamic chambers [[Citation16](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Using the dynamic open chamber approach, sampling flow rates are reduced (2-10 L min−1), increasing the sampling area (1 m2), and pre-concentrating the sample with an effective sorption trap [[Citation17](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. A 1 m2 plate that is positioned a few centimeters above the soil’s surface has a pump attached to it that draws gas through the sorption trap. Due to the high surface area to flow rate ratio, little change in gas concentration occurs as sample volumes are decreased by analyte sorption. Open chambers are easier to use than closed ones. However, the constant airflow and the pressure differences between the atmosphere and the chamber’s headspace are major limitations [[Citation18](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)].
The most used technique for determining gas exchange between the soil and atmosphere is the closed-chamber approach. It is frequently utilized in wetlands as well as other ecosystems because of its low cost and ease of usage, especially in places without access to electricity. Flux measurements can be made frequently to determine diurnal, seasonal or annual fluxes. Sampling with closed chambers often enables the determination of extremely minute concentration changes when combined with precise analytical techniques [[Citation19](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Closed chambers can be mobile or permanently built to allow numerous sample locations. Throughout an experiment, gas samples are drawn from the chamber using syringes or sampling ports, and they are then directly quantified using methods like gas chromatograph (GC) [[Citation20](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Gas chambers are made up of basic units that measure 25 × 25 × 15 cm3 and are 0.64 cm thick acrylic plastic. This plastic can be dark or transparent. Generally, the transparent chambers need to be covered to minimize the rise in temperature when installed in the field. Each base unit is positioned in the soil at a depth of about 10 cm. The tops (25 × 25 × 15 cm3) are sealed to the chamber bases using water. Gas bubbles contained in the soil are prevented from being disturbed and released by the top’s simple removal. The temperature of the chamber is monitored by a thermometer, and the sample port is a rubber septum. After placing the closed chamber above the base unit for a brief length of time, the technique is suitable for measuring gas fluxes. To assure mixing in huge static chambers, a small fan is often used. Gas samples are collected using a 15 mL polypropylene syringe and a 20-gauge stainless steel hypodermic needle. Samples are obtained after 0, 10, 20, and 30 min of chamber placement (or another appropriate time interval to monitor the buildup of the gas concentration). An exetainer/vial (6, 9 or 12 mL) is refilled with an additional 2-3 mL of the sample gas. By creating an overpressure, contamination of the sample gases with outside gas is prevented. The rubber septum of the vial’s injection hole is sealed with silicone sealant. A GC can be used to determine the concentrations of CH4, CO2, or N2O in the gas samples. The results collected from the GC are used to compute the exchange of gases between the soil surface and atmosphere. Time of sampling should take into account seasonal and nocturnal dynamics. Generally a huge variety of chamber designs and sample procedures are used [[Citation21](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Closed dynamic chambers can be connected to online gas analyzers to measure GHG concentrations continuously.
Closed dynamic chambers operate on a similar premise to closed static chambers; however, they are used in conjunction with an analyzer outside the chamber. Compact nondispersive infrared (NDIR) sensors for CO2 can be installed on closed dynamic chambers [[Citation15](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation22](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Closed-chamber methods are a simple, low-cost technology that may be used to estimate GHG fluxes in a variety of agroecosystems between the soil and the atmosphere. GHG fluxes in a variety of agroecosystems between the soil and atmosphere. Since they can be easily and inexpensively constructed from a variety of materials in a variety of styles, shapes, and sizes, closed chambers are best suited for some activities. Since they can be easily and inexpensively constructed from a variety of materials in a variety of styles, shapes, and sizes, closed chambers are often preferred. The method allows for the measurement of GHG from various locations at different times using the same equipment and staff because the operation of the chambers and the evaluation are uncomplicated. Without an onsite power supply, with closed chambers very low rates of GHG fluxes, depending on the sensitivity of the analyzer, can be obtained for pasture, cropland, rice paddy, wetland, drain, and ditches in a short period (between 30 min and an hour). The closed chamber technique, however, has some limitations. Rising gas concentrations in the confined headspace cause nonlinear fluxes between the soil and the air because they lower the concentration gradient and, as a result, gas diffusion. Atmospheric pressure irregularities are modulated (or even eliminated) in closed chambers; nevertheless, the air pressure within and outside the chamber can be regulated by using customized vents. Alterations in temperature are conceivable in the chamber’s atmosphere as well as the soil. It is feasible to lessen these temperature fluctuations by sealing the chamber with an insulating and reflecting material.
Static closed chamber methods are typically used to quantify the gaseous flow from soil to the atmosphere. This is the most widely used technique for measuring GHG fluxes for soil and manure surfaces. Static chambers placed on the soil surface will measure the gas fluxes for a time period that is typically not exceeding 60 min. At regular intervals throughout chamber deployment, samples of the chamber headspace gas will be taken and kept for later gas chromatography analysis. In the static closed chambers approach, soil surface gas emissions fluxes are computed by tracking the variation in gas concentration over time in the chamber headspace inside the chamber. The majority of data describing soil-based fluxes of GHGs are from static chamber techniques, which have been widely used across both managed and natural environments [[Citation23](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation24](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The sampling apparatus functions by enclosing a known volume of soil (0.5 m2) in a closed chamber that permits soil gases to enter the chamber headspace [[Citation25](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The desired gas is concentrated in the headspace for a certain amount of time (closure time) while the valve is closed. Gas concentration fluctuations in the chamber headspace are measured and converted into a flux rate, which represents the flux into or out of the soil [[Citation21](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The air inside the chamber is manually collected using a syringe or container, and it is then subjected to GC analysis in the lab [[Citation26–32](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)].
To account for the spatial variability over uneven terrain, and in homogenous soils (e.g. under grazing) an adequate number of sampling spots are required. Closed chambers that are simple to manufacture, and do not require the use of extremely sensitive, accurate, or quick analytical techniques are typically used in situation where large number of measurement spots are required [[Citation8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. They are inexpensive to make and numerous chambers can be utilized simultaneously for a representative sample area [[Citation33](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Closed static and dynamic chambers are available in both manual and automatic configurations, with the difference in how the chamber’s headspace is ventilated after each measurement.
Automated open chambers have been developed and tested e.g. for volatile organic chemicals (VOCs), CO2, NO, NO2, N2O, NH3 [[Citation14](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Automatic chambers can contain a sliding lid that is opened and closed by a pneumatic system to enable gas exchange, eliminating any need for a constant operator presence [[Citation15](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Similar to static chambers, automated chambers should sample several spatial locations to cover a representative area in the field. The system includes infrared gas analyzers, which is beneficial as an automatic chamber receives a good sample frequency and can more accurately integrate fluxes over time with little error. A reduction in manual work and the ability to record potential emissions changes are further benefits [[Citation34](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Automatic chambers allow for much more data over a longer research period, but due to their cost and running needs and multiple chambers being linked to one analyzer, this method is only appropriate for small areas [[Citation33](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation35–38](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Another major problem is that while conducting simultaneous GHG measurements, closure time should be carefully adjusted.
Large research regions cannot be covered by chambers. Additionally, because air flows through the window and disperses gases into the atmosphere, it is often not possible to apply convection-related gas emissions using chamber methods [[Citation39](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. When covering huge areas, tunnels may be a way to get around these restrictions. To investigate GHG emissions from the trash in open compost windows, a tunnel method was used with a surface area of roughly 50 m2, measured over 10 m in length, 5 m in breadth, and 1.5 to 2 m in height. The tunnel is ventilated by two ventilators from one side, and fresh air enters the tunnel from the front. A 4 mm diameter Teflon tube is used to sample the air, which is then injected into an infrared gas N2O and CH4 analyzer. The GHG are released into the fresh air as it exits the tunnel at the rear. A GC can be used in a lab to measure the air samples. However, the infrared radiation (IR) analyzers are typically not as precise and accurate as laser-based techniques which are currently used by Picarro, and also Licor who have released laser techniques for all GHGs. The benefit of chamber methods is their simplicity of installation and lack of need for high precision or quick analytical methods [[Citation8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation40](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. It is important to remember that inappropriately using chambers disturbs the soil and changes the soil’s microclimate. Animal gas emissions can be measured accurately in chambers; however, the method can obstruct the natural production of gases. When long-range micrometeorological approaches are not viable, chamber techniques are frequently effective for smaller settings, including porous media in agricultural and industrial contexts [[Citation14](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. They often cost less to install due to the less sophisticated equipment required. In paired animal research, chambers are effective for comparing the gas emissions from ruminants fed various diets, evaluating additives for reducing CH4 emissions, and documenting genetic changes across individuals. The early assessment of global GHG inventories from different ecosystems has benefited greatly from the use of chamber methods. Recent improvements to gas detectors and other tower-based techniques have made chamber methods a vital addition to effectively minimizing uncertainty in the ecosystems’ GHGs budget al.though chamber experiments have produced useful data, they have drawbacks. The primary issue with chamber techniques is that they create an artificial, constrained environment, which can alter the boundary conditions for the generation of natural gas [[Citation23](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation41](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Chamber approaches can cover a huge range of spaces up to entire animal houses. Because of interruptions to the transport systems, animal variability, and many other factors, gaseous emissions from bigger chambers are associated with errors [[Citation42](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Chambers can cause animal discomfort and produce an artificial habitat that biases emissions. Manual chambers are labor intensive, which reduces the number of data that can be obtained, but because they are less expensive, are large number of sites can be sampled [[Citation43](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation44](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. According to differences caused by chamber type, flow rates, and flow configuration [[Citation23](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation42](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)], chambers are generally better suited for mitigation studies rather than choosing emission factors for assessing GHG inventories.
### Micrometeorological methods
Micrometeorological approaches integrate GHG emissions from greater surface area, minimizing the difficulties associated with the spatial variability. The fundamental idea of micrometeorological methodologies used to assess GHG fluxes to or from the surface of the soil is that gas transport occurs through the atmosphere’s eddying motion, forcing air to move in portions from one level to another. It is possible to estimate the GHG fluxes by measuring the velocities and concentrations of turbulence components. GHGs including NH3, have been measured by utilizing micrometeorological methods from both point and non-point sources [[Citation3](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation4](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation45](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The mass balance approach, dispersion modeling, flux gradient connections, or eddy covariance theory may all provide the foundation for micrometeorological techniques [[Citation12](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation42](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. In these methods, gas sensors are mounted on towers to record wind, temperature, and gas concentration readings at one or more spots above the soil or grassland surface [[Citation8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Improvements in analyzer technology now enable continuous field-scaled observations of GHGs. Over the past ten years, standardization techniques have been established, enabling comparability of gas flux data across locations, ecosystem types, and climatic areas [[Citation46](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation47](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)].
Continuous measurements of GHG fluxes are possible using micrometeorological techniques with little disruption to the ecosystem under investigation, with several advantages over conventional chamber systems. Long-term studies and larger areas (fetch) can be examined. #### Flux-gradient methods
By measuring the gas concentrations at two or more distinct heights and logging the horizontal wind speed, the flux-gradient method streamlines the observations required to calculate the vertical flux. Herein the vertical concentration gradient and turbulent diffusivity are combined to produce the vertical flux. The vertical concentration gradient is estimated using data from two different heights, and the turbulent diffusivity is calculated using wind speed and the turbulence readings. The measurements of the energy exchange at the surface as well as the vertical concentrations of the gas, air temperature, and humidity can also be used to compute the vertical flux [[Citation42](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. It is crucial to employ standard instruments for reading gas concentrations from various heights for applying flux-gradient procedures with high accuracy [[Citation8](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Although there are some distinctions, the flux-gradient hypothesis presupposes that molecular diffusion and turbulent gas transfer are the same process. While molecular diffusion originates from the random movement of molecules, turbulent transfer occurs when air parcels move from one level to another. The wind speed, height above the surface (such as soil, plant canopy, or water), vertical temperature gradient, and aerodynamic roughness of the surface all play a role in determining the actual magnitudes. Conventionally, if the concentration rises toward the surface, the gradient is positive; if not, it is negative. Measurements inside the constant flux layer of gradients with the height of wind speed, concentration of trace gases, and air temperature provide fundamental information for several ways of determining vertical fluxes across broad surfaces.

#### Aerodynamic method
The basis of the aerodynamic approach is the relationship between the momentum flux equation and the wind speed gradient. This method calls for the measurement of the wind speed and the gradient of the gas concentration at two or more heights. Vertical air density gradients caused by heat and water vapor fluxes affect the trace gas flux, hence stability corrections are necessary. The method is used for gases that can be examined directly in a stream of flowing fluid. On a gas sampling mast, gas streams are drawn through sampling tubes at various heights while wind speed and temperature are recorded at various altitudes.
#### Bowen ratio/energy balance method
The Bowen ratio/energy balance approach for calculating vertical fluxes is one of the most popular methods. The Bowen ratio is the ratio of sensible to latent heat fluxes. Because this method is based on the energy balance at the surface, measurements of the wind velocity profile are not necessary. The inward net radiation is composed of the soil heat flux, sensible heat flux, and latent heat flux. To apply this technique, it is necessary to measure the vertical gradients of temperature, humidity, and gas concentration. These data give approximations for the fluxes of water vapor, trace gases, and sensible heat without adding the uncertainty and complexity of stability corrections. The rate at which water evaporates can also be calculated using this technique. At any time, but especially when examining the diurnal pattern of trace gas loss, the use of both aerodynamic and energy-balancing approaches would seem to be advisable. The significant net radiation fluxes required by energy balancing methods are the biggest and most significant limitation for trace gas fluxes. ### Cavity ring-down spectroscopy
The monitoring of GHGs almost rely on optical methods of some kind. No matter the optical approach used or how stable the device may be, all require careful regulation of the molecules’ temperature and pressure. All optical-based procedures also necessitate the precise selection and management of the light wavelengths used in order to measure only the molecules of interest. The method employed to measure the absorption of light by the molecules of interest must essentially have no systematic effects that cause optical absorption uncertainties if the highest level of precision is to be achieved. Temperature gradients are likely to exist in uncontrolled sample chambers, which could cause large mistakes in concentration readings. It can be challenging to account for such gradients, especially when ambient temperature changes occur more quickly than the sample chamber’s thermal equilibrium can be reached. Incorporating gas standards inside the analyzer has been suggested by certain manufacturers as a solution to the analyzers’ shortcomings. However, GHG standards are expensive, and even the regular introduction of such standards does not guarantee the analyzer’s reliable functionality in between measurements of gas standards. Furthermore, it would be very expensive and probably impractical to maintain a big fleet of GHG standards if a wide-scale network were to be built out. The wavelength of absorption filters and lasers can change with temperature and time. By absorbing light from molecules other than those of interest, such drifts might result in systematic mistakes. There is no method to verify that some light is not being absorbed by an "interfering" gas when a laser or absorption filter wanders. The advent of Picarro’s cavity ringdown technology has drastically streamlined the process of taking measurements of GHGs and assuring the highest possible quality data.
When a gas sample is injected into a high-finesse optical cavity, its optical absorbance is measured to determine its concentration or the desired isotopic ratio, known as cavity ring-down spectroscopy (CRDS). A simple CRDS consists of a laser, a photo-detector, and a high precision optical cavity with three mirrors. Through one partially reflecting mirror, laser light is operationally injected into the cavity. Using a second partially reflecting mirror, the photo-detector outside the cavity then gauges the rise in light intensity inside the cavity over time. The "ring-down" measurement is performed by abruptly turning off the laser and observing the light intensity in the cavity as it exponentially decays with a time constant that is influenced by absorption and scattering of measured sample. The majority of the light is trapped inside the cavity after the laser is turned off for a considerable amount of time, creating an effective path length through the sample of tens of kilometers. The optical absorbance per unit length is absolutely measured by CRDS (calculated from the ringdown time _via_ the speed of light). Two high reliability, telecom-grade distributed feedback (DFB) lasers are used in the CRDS GHG analyzer. A single CO2 spectral feature is measured by one laser at a wavelength of 1603 nm, while CH4 and H2O vapor spectral features are measured by the other laser at a wavelength of 1651 nm. Using an optical switch, light from the DFB lasers is picked and sent across a polarization-maintaining optical cable to a very precise wavelength monitor. The analyzer uses a unique traveling wave cavity and a proprietary wavelength monitor to detect optical absorption while adjusting the temperature and pressure of the ambient air sample that is continuously flowing through the optical cavity.

Stable isotope 15N or 13C can be used to figure out GHG gas fluxes that are impossible to measure or are challenging to measure using any non-isotopic method. Microbiologists have taken advantage of the novel opportunities that 15N can offer to estimate the turnover rates of individual processes in the N cycle utilizing dilution principles. Without utilizing complex 15N tracking methods [[Citation91](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)], it is impossible to determine which processes contribute to overall N2O emissions.
The 15N gas flux technique needs to uniformly label the soil with 15N, and it is not sensitive enough to detect small N2 fluxes in a natural atmosphere [[Citation92](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The final phase of microbial denitrification (N2O reduction to N2) is the anoxic reduction of nitrate (NO3-) to dinitrogen (N2) with the intermediaries NO2, NO, and N2O. Although the application of the HeO2 approach for direct measurement of N2 fluxes is constrained by the requirement for specialized gas-tight incubation systems, it is not susceptible to the issues with 15N-based methods [[Citation93](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The 15N gas flux method estimates the quantity of N2 or N2O released from 15N labeled NO3- given to the soil in order to compute the fluxes from conventional denitrification, where N2 and N2O are formed by the fusion of two NO precursor molecules. Experimental soil is enriched with NO3- that is strongly 15N enriched to measure canonical denitrification. The 15N gases produced are collected in sealed chambers, and the 15N emission is determined by the relative abundance of N2 and N2O isotopologues inside. The 15N GFM is a successful approach for spotting the emergence of hybrid N2 and N2O fluxes. Although it is relevant in both the lab and the field, it is dependent on several presumptions and requirements that are not necessarily simple, feasible, or true. Limitations of the 15N GFM include the problem of non-homogenous 15N enrichment of the NO3- pool, incomplete recovery of denitrification gases, and inaccurate definition of the soil volume represented by denitrification measurements.

### 3.6. Satellite measurements of atmospheric GHGs concentrations
Climate change evaluation has substantially advanced owing to satellite-based measurements of GHGs. The ability of satellites to provide constant observational qualities with worldwide coverage of the Earth’s surface and atmosphere allows for a more accurate revelation of the spatiotemporal variation in the atmospheric GHGs concentrations. At both a regional and global level, it is also widely used as a primary source to monitor changes in atmospheric CO2 concentration [[Citation114–116](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Estimates of GHGs emissions resulting from land use changes [[Citation117](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)] and deforestation can be derived using global land cover and forest biomass measurements, which is not achievable with ground-based surveys alone. A sizable amount of CO2 is dissolved in the seas and exchanged between the atmosphere and the surface ocean as a result of temperature changes, ocean mixing, and marine animal photosynthesis and respiration. Because plants in the northern hemisphere absorb CO2 during photosynthesis in the summer and release it through respiration in the winter, the concentration of CO2 varies seasonally. As a result of chemical reactions destroying CH4 more quickly in the summer, concentrations of CH4 are low. The global coverage required to determine the geographic variation of energy consumption, quantify the GHGs emissions from fires, and identify broad patterns and trends in ecosystem productivity is provided by remote sensing of nighttime brightness, biological activity, and fires. With the use of satellite measurements, top-down and bottom-up estimates of GHGs emissions can be improved. Satellites will also likely give important observations that will aid in the monitoring and comprehension of changes to the Earth’s surface and atmosphere. Strong anthropogenic emission sources, like as megacities and high-density metropolitan areas, can be identified through satellite data as areas where CO2 levels have changed.
By using satellite measurements, atmospheric gas concentrations are determined using a gas’s capacity to absorb electromagnetic radiation at specific wavelengths. Some of these devices can also use radiation that is emitted by the planet itself or from lasers that are mounted on the satellite, but most of them use solar radiation that is reflected off the Earth’s surface. Satellite-based spectrometers detect CO2 and CH4 at a variety of wavelengths in accordance with their absorption spectra. Shorter near-infrared wavelength satellites have shown to be the most effective in detecting surface emissions since they are responsive to CO2 concentrations close to the surface [[Citation118](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Other satellites have measured CO2 concentrations in the mid-troposphere, which is situated 6 to 11 km above the Earth’s surface, using longer thermal infrared wavelengths. The measurement of absorption in three near-infrared wavelength bands is necessary for the typical CO2 content in a vertical column that extends from the surface of the Earth to the upper atmosphere. The weak and strong CO2 absorption bands are located around 1.58 and 2.06 μm, respectively, whereas the molecular oxygen (O2) A-band is located around 0.76 μm [[Citation119](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The same methods used for CO2 observations are also used to measure the column CH4 content. CH4 absorbs light at wavelengths around 1.67 m, which is the basis for the measurements. Clouds along with the CO2 and CH4 spectrometer are simultaneously measured by satellites that are used to detect the amounts of atmospheric GHGs, allowing data impacted by clouds to be marked and rejected.
Flux inversions use satellite CO2 observations to estimate CO2 exchanges between terrestrial ecosystems and the ocean, but not to estimate CO2 emissions from fossil fuels. To differentiate between CO2 produced by burning fossil fuels and the impacts of natural CO2 fluxes, satellite CO2 data can be combined with supplementary observations. The emission ratios of CO and NOx to CO2 during fossil fuel combustion can be used to estimate the quantity of CO2 generated from fossil fuels [[Citation120](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527),[Citation121](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Additionally, ground-based tracer observations such as radiocarbon (14C) in CO2 might be integrated with satellite-based CO2 data. Satellites cannot detect radiocarbon, but ground-based and airborne measurements can estimate CO2 generated from fossil fuels by assessing the dilution of 14C in comparison to a reference level [[Citation122](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Warmer temperatures and increased precipitation cause CH4 fluxes in wetlands to rise. Due to the constraints of the currently available satellite data, CH4 flux estimations are subject to significant uncertainty, just like CO2 flux inversions. By integrating satellite estimates of the burned area with model-based estimations of the amount of biomass burned, GHGs emissions from fires can be calculated. Tropical wetland flows are stronger and more varied than previously estimated, according to recent SCIAMACHY observations [[Citation123](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. A variety of emissions and their regional distribution can be estimated using data from satellite views of the Earth’s surface, which includes information on land cover, forest biomass, fires, biological productivity, and human energy use. Sentinel 2 A and 2B from the ESA, which launched in 2015 and 2016 respectively, complemented Landsat 8's 2013 launch to provide sub-weekly temporal resolution. These satellites capture high-resolution resolution (10–60 m) images of the Earth’s surface using visible and near-infrared infrared wavelengths of light. Sentinel satellites can be effective allies since a variety of platforms, including OCO-2, OCO-3, METOP-A, TROPOMI (Sentinel-5P), TANSAT, GOSAT, and GOSAT-2, currently offer high-resolution GHG measurements. The goal of GHGat is to establish itself as the standard for measuring GHGs emissions remotely from any source in the earth. No other commercial or government satellite initiative particularly mentions the detection and measurement of GHG emissions from point sources as small as individual oil and gas wells. The GHGSat constellation currently consists of six satellites. Wide-angle Fabry-Perot (WAF-P) imaging spectrometers are included on each satellite to determine the vertical column abundances of GHGs. Although GHGSat-D and GHGSat-C1 can measure both CH4 and CO2, the complete constellation is designed to measure CH4. The WAF-P Imaging Spectrometer is the main sensor on all GHGS at satellites. A hypercube created by this primary sensor is made up of a stack of overlapping, spectrally picked images that were taken in less than 30 s. For each ground pixel, this hypercube embeds data corresponding to hundreds of wavelengths from the top-of-atmosphere spectral radiance. The hypercube is adjusted for sensor responsiveness and instrument optics after downloading. A measurement model that accounts for surface, instrument, and atmospheric contributions to light is then used to get information on the gas column density and surface reflection for each ground pixel. The Field of View (FOV) of these datasets, which is approximately 12 km x 12 km, enables the capture and differentiation of gas plumes emitted from anthropogenic processes from background concentrations, thereby allowing for differential measurement. The spatial resolution of GHGSat satellites is normally 25 m (GHGSat-D has a resolution of > 50 m). The abundance datasets from many observations of a particular location can be used to create a monitoring product for the semi-automatic detection of emitter activity or substantial changes in emission rates using assessment measures. By merging the reflectance and abundance products, a "Concentration Map" with exceptional human readability can be produced.
The orbits used by the current satellites that measure CO2 and CH4 are about 600 km above the Earth’s surface and pass very near the poles. Geostationary satellites keep a consistent eye on a specific area by orbiting the Earth at an altitude of around 36,000 km, keeping their orbital period in sync with the rotation of the planet. Geostationary satellites can be very effective for monitoring urban areas because they offer many observations each day [[Citation124](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. A network of Fourier Transform Spectrometers on the ground called the Total Column Carbon Observing Network (TCCON) delivers precise measurements of column CO2, CH4, and other gases [[Citation125](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. Validating satellite observations is one of the key goals of TCCON [[Citation126](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. The instrument’s properties can alter, and the satellite’s orbital parameters can drift over time. Airborne observations of the composition of the atmosphere were used to calibrate the column readings taken at several TCCON stations. TCCON is extremely helpful for satellite measurements over land, however there is a need for better calibration of readings over water [[Citation127](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. To monitor column-averaged CO2 and/or CH4 concentrations, three satellite missions (SCIAMACHY, GOSAT, and OCO-2) have been launched. From 2002 to 2012, the Scanning Imaging Absorption Monitoring Spectrometer for Atmospheric Chartography (SCIAMACHY) satellite of the European Space Agency (ESA) was in service. With a geographical resolution of 30 × 60 km2 and precision of 2–5 ppm in CO2 and 30–50 ppb in CH4, SCIAMACHY delivered the earliest record of total column CO2 and CH4 observations. In the standard operation, SCIAMACHY's swath was 960 km wide and every six days it completed full global coverage in nadir viewing mode. Greenhouse Gases Observing Satellite (GOSAT) was the first satellite dedicated to the monitoring of GHGs in the atmosphere. Japanese Ministry of the Environment (MOE), National Institute for Environmental Studies (NIES), and Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA) jointly launched and operated the GOSAT in 2009. A spectrometer (TANSO-FTS) and a cloud aerosol imager (TANSO-CAI) are both included with GOSAT [[Citation128](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. In order to cover the entire earth in three days, GOSAT typically takes three to five footprints each measuring 10 km in diameter over its 700 km swath and CO2 measurement precision is about 2-3 ppm where CH4 is about 15 ppb. The Orbiting Carbon Observatory 2 (OCO-2) was launched by the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in 2014.OCO-2 has a narrower swath of 10 km but a greater precision of about 0.5 ppm and high spatial resolution (1 × 1.5 km2). Every 16 days, OCO-2 completes a near-global coverage, however, because of its limited swath, measurements are not taken in some regions. The nadir and glint viewing modes on OCO-2 can be adjusted. The device is directed downwards toward the surface while in nadir viewing mode to measure the air column just beneath the satellite’s absorption of reflected sunlight. Nadir measurements are not achievable over dark surfaces, such as water, because the readings rely on reflected sunlight. The device is oriented to point at the bright glint spot on the Earth’s surface where the specular reflection of solar radiation happens, during glint viewing mode. Additionally, OCO-2 features a unique target mode that focuses measurement on a particular region, like a TCCON site.
Several access portals offer the public free access to satellite data. Envisat and Earth Explorer missions are accessible through the ESA's Earth Online service. All data derived from satellite missions is accessible through NASA's Earth data portal. An interface gateway for GOSAT has been developed by JAXA and NIES. The essential climate variables, which include atmospheric CO2 and CH4 concentrations, are consistently estimated by the Copernicus Climate Change Service. These estimates are supported by the EU's Greenhouse Gas Climate Change Initiative, which also plans to provide high-quality global datasets of X CO2 and X CH4 derived from SCIAMACHY and GOSAT observations. With the help of satellite data, NASA's Carbon Monitoring System is working on a series of initiatives to examine carbon flows and provide data products that can be used by academics, government officials, and the business sector. National and regional organizations like ESA, NASA, and JAXA coordinate the planning of new satellite missions. These organizations have received assistance from corresponding expert bodies, including Copernicus and the US National Research Council, on CO2 and CH4 emissions monitoring strategies as well as additional components including ground-based observations and bottom-up inventories [[Citation129](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17583004.2024.2366527)]. States may use satellite-enabled measurement data to check their own emissions inventory reports or as an initial point for the globally coordinated expert review process.
## Discussion and recommendation
Major frameworks for measuring gas emissions, such as chamber-based, mass balance, micrometeorological methodologies, isotopic techniques, Picarro gas analyzer method and satellite-based measurements have been reviewed along with related analytical and sensing methods. This article provides a comprehensive overview of the most suitable methods for GHG emission measurements, focusing on advancements in technology, instrumentations, ecological suitability and analytical techniques. The researcher’s requirements and land use, including the spatial/temporal variability, the availability of operators and equipment, the limits on the sampling sites, the targets or funding and maintenance expenditure mainly determine which methodology is the most useful one. Chamber methods (both closed and open) involve enclosing an area to measure gas concentrations changes over time. Chamber methods are widely used to estimate the gas emissions from agricultural and animal feeding activities, though without a power supply, closed chambers can measure very low rates of GHG fluxes for pasture, cropland, rice paddy, wetland, drain, and ditches in a short period. Since they can be easily and inexpensively constructed from a variety of materials in a variety of styles, shapes, and sizes, closed chambers are best suited for some activities. The method allows for the measurement of GHG from various locations at different times using the same equipment and staff because the operation of the chambers and the evaluation are uncomplicated. Rising gas concentrations in the confined headspace cause nonlinear fluxes between the soil and the air, nevertheless, the air pressure within and outside the chamber can be regulated by using customized apertures. According to differences caused by chamber type, flow rates, and flow configurations, chambers are generally better suited for mitigation studies rather than choosing emission factors (data fusion, numerical modeling) for assessing GHG inventories. However, chamber methods are suitable for small-scale studies but may not representative for larger areas. Eddy covariance directly measures the exchange of gases in systems (between the atmosphere and soil), continuous data can be found but require sophisticated equipment and expertise. The mass balance approach, dispersion modeling, flux gradient connections, or eddy covariance theory provide the basis for micrometeorological techniques. Most developing countries’ primary disadvantages with micrometeorological procedures are that they lack the expertise and understanding of these approaches, which are usually more costly and require specific analytical knowledge, expensive monitoring tools, and trained personnel for a successful application.
Micrometeorological approaches are useful for measurements from broader footprints, whereas chamber methods are frequently used to detect gas emissions from point sources. The best choice for accurate measurements of GHGs is the use of stable isotopic methods but it is very challenging to precisely estimate GHG emissions across multiple agroecosystems and identify their microbiological sources. CRDS gas analyzer can concurrently detect the atmospheric concentrations of GHGs at the ppm level with incredibly low drift and great precision. Observations from satellites provide valuable information on GHGs emissions. Even without permission from the area being viewed, satellite observations are potent tool for remote monitoring. It offers global coverage by monitoring remote areas but limited efficiency due to challenges in special resolution and interference from clouds. However, these limitations can be overcome by using infrared gas emission measurement as it uses infrared cameras to visualize and quantify the emitted gas, effective in leak detection with the cons of gas specificity and dependency on environmental conditions. Herein, gas specificity properly utilizes sensor techniques providing high specificity for individual gas species i.e. NDIR specially used for CO2 detection, but it is a costly method. Advancements in GHG emission measurements involve a combination of direct measurement methods, remote sensing techniques, advanced sensor technologies, flux measurements and modeling approaches. The optimal method depends on the specific goals, scale and resources availability for a particular study. Integrating these techniques and fostering collaboration between researchers, industry and policymakers will contribute to a more accurate and holistic understanding of GHG emissions.
## Conclusions
This review is purely methodological one which, on purpose, tried to familiarize the reader with the vast range of different developments and is neither exhaustive nor intended to be a systematic review. Depending on the requirements and the gas of interest, each methodology has pros and cons. The inability to use analytical instruments for in-situ measurements due to their high cost and complexity necessitates the use of a laboratory to determine the gas concentrations in samples taken from field chambers. More precise knowledge of GHG emissions over vast areas would be made possible by a low-cost, easily accessible sensor that can gather emission data over a large area. However, it is quite difficult to develop such an analytical method. Choosing the best procedure and suggesting standard methodologies, is challenging when comparing various techniques. To assure quality controls and to enable scientists to compare the gas emission features of particular farming, manure and slurry management, territories, and organisms, it would be highly beneficial to have coordinated standards and guidelines. The capabilities, constraints, and application of several currently used measurement techniques and technologies used to estimate gas emissions from agricultural operations have been thoroughly analyzed and discussed in this review. The article discusses strategies for measuring GHG emissions from different agroecosystems using isotopic and other methodologies, which may also be used to measure climate-smart agriculture policies to reduce GHG emissions. The methods described in this article are on purpose and easy-to-understand and the information should be helpful for scientists who require knowledge of current approaches to get a better understanding.
