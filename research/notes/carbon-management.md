ABSTRACT 
Fossil fuels, land use, and agriculture are the three major sources contributing to the rise in 
atmospheric greenhouse gases (GHGs). Agricultural activities including the production of 
rice, animal raising, and aquaculture directly contribute to GHG emissions. Approximately 
one fourth of all GHG emissions are attributable to agricultural operations primarily as a 
result of unsustainable farming practices. The GHG emissions from agriculture include carbon 
dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxide (N2O), methane (CH4). However, measurements of GHGs are 
laborious, expensive, and technically challenging but are crucial to select the most appropri­
ate and feasible system. Since it is difficult to find a comprehensive overview of the variety 
of techniques, including latest technologies, in one article, this review aims to provide details 
of available methodologies, their benefits and drawbacks, with a focus on those that have 
been extensively tested in various ecosystems and regions. This paper is a purely methodo­
logical one which, on purpose, tried to familiarize the reader with the vast range of different 
developments. Our analysis is not exhaustive and is not intended to be a systematic review. 
The article summarizes that micrometeorological approaches are suitable for measurement 
from broader footprints, whereas chamber techniques measuring gases from point sources, 
are more appropriate for high spatio-temporal resolutions in diverse ecosystems. Stable iso­
tope techniques are the most accurate methods and allow a process-specific quantification 
of GHGs but require sophisticated equipment.

Introduction
Measurements of greenhouse gases (GHGs) from 
agricultural soils, manure, and animal feeding 
activities are challenging and often unreliable, 
associated with large uncertainties. Despite that 
the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change 
(IPCC) methodology is the most widely used and 
allows for comparisons [1], GHG emissions from 
the same sector differ between countries due to 
differences in handling, and use of inputs, as well 
as variations in the calculation and analysis meth­

might not work well for a mixture of gases.

Measurement of greenhouse gas emissions
It is imperative to comprehend the contributions 
of both human activities and natural processes on 
the radiative properties of the atmosphere. 
Disproportionate emissions are related to certain 
sources (cattle, poultry, crop lands, fossil fuel burn­
ing etc.). Land use contributed about 25% of all 
land-use emissions [6]. Estimation of the GHGs 
flow and the evaluation of effective remedies for 
reducing emissions are based on field measure­
ments [7]. To accurately assess GHGs emissions in 
agricultural fields and aquatic systems, a variety of 
measurement strategies, each having strengths 
and constraints [8–10] are available. Chamber- 
based techniques and micrometeorological tech­
niques are the two most widely used meth­
ods [8,11].
Chamber methods
The main techniques for measuring GHGs from 
agricultural areas are chamber-based methods. It is 
the most popular and widely used method for 
monitoring gas emissions from soil surfaces using 
chambers, which allows gases of interest to gather 
in a defined volume. Chamber methods are recog­
nized as the most widely used to estimate the gas 
emissions from agricultural and animal feeding 
activities [12]. Chamber-based techniques sampled 
from a constrained above-ground headspace and 
concentrate on changes in gas concentration at 
the soil surface. Their high coefficients of variation 
are caused by the spatial variance in soil gas fluxes 
however, they allow for many treatments and 
small-area assessments. Chamber sizes can vary 
from less than 1 m3 to more than 150 m3. To meas­
ure the changes in CO2, N2O, and CH4 concentra­
tion, 
samples 
are 
taken 
at 
several 
times 
throughout chamber closure. The effect of cham­
ber size and deployment time determines the level 
of accuracy that can be achieved [13].
There are two different kinds of chambers: 
steady state (SS), where the gas loop is open and 
the concentration of the measured gas does not 
increase over time, and non-steady state (NSS), 
where the gas loop is closed and the gas concen­
trations in the chamber increase. The conditions 
inside the chamber should not be different to the 
outside conditions (e.g. temperature, pressure) to 
avoid artefacts [12,14]. Thus appropriate insulation 
and vents that allow transmission of pressure dif­
ferences are recommended. To avoid artefacts due 
to insertion of the chambers into the soil frames 
should be pre-installed on which the chamber can 
be placed.

Static closed chamber methods are typically 
used to quantify the gaseous flow from soil to the 
atmosphere. This is the most widely used tech­
nique for measuring GHG fluxes for soil and 
manure surfaces. Static chambers placed on the 
soil surface will measure the gas fluxes for a time 
period that is typically not exceeding 60 min. At 
regular intervals throughout chamber deployment, 
samples of the chamber headspace gas will be 
taken and kept for later gas chromatography ana­
lysis. In the static closed chambers approach, soil 
surface gas emissions fluxes are computed by 
tracking the variation in gas concentration over 
time in the chamber headspace inside the cham­
ber. The majority of data describing soil-based 
fluxes of GHGs are from static chamber techniques, 
which have been widely used across both man­
aged and natural environments [23,24]. The sam­
pling apparatus functions by enclosing a known 
volume of soil (0.5 m2) in a closed chamber that 
permits soil gases to enter the chamber headspace 
[25]. The desired gas is concentrated in the head­
space for a certain amount of time (closure time) 
while the valve is closed. Gas concentration fluctu­
ations in the chamber headspace are measured 
and converted into a flux rate, which represents 
the flux into or out of the soil [21]. The air inside 
the chamber is manually collected using a syringe 
or container, and it is then subjected to GC ana­
lysis in the lab [26–32].
To account for the spatial variability over 
uneven terrain, and in homogenous soils (e.g. 
under grazing) an adequate number of sampling 
spots are required. Closed chambers that are sim­
ple to manufacture, and do not require the use of 
extremely sensitive, accurate, or quick analytical 
techniques are typically used in situation where 
large number of measurement spots are required 
[8]. They are inexpensive to make and numerous 
chambers can be utilized simultaneously for a rep­
resentative sample area [33]. Closed static and 
dynamic chambers are available in both manual 
and automatic configurations, with the difference 
in how the chamber’s headspace is ventilated after 
each measurement.
Automated open chambers have been devel­
oped and tested e.g. for volatile organic chemicals 
(VOCs), CO2, NO, NO2, N2O, NH3 [14]. Automatic 
chambers can contain a sliding lid that is opened 
and closed by a pneumatic system to enable gas 
exchange, eliminating any need for a constant 
operator presence [15]. Similar to static chambers, 
automated chambers should sample several spatial 
locations to cover a representative area in the 
field. The system includes infrared gas analyzers, 
which is beneficial as an automatic chamber 
receives a good sample frequency and can more 
accurately integrate fluxes over time with little 
error. A reduction in manual work and the ability 
to record potential emissions changes are further 
benefits [34]. Automatic chambers allow for much 
more data over a longer research period, but due 
to their cost and running needs and multiple 
chambers being linked to one analyzer, this 
method is only appropriate for small areas 
[33,35–38]. Another major problem is that while 
conducting simultaneous GHG measurements, 
closure time should be carefully adjusted.
Large research regions cannot be covered by 
chambers. Additionally, because air flows through 
the window and disperses gases into the atmos­

[39]. When covering huge areas, tunnels may be a

length, 5 m in breadth, and 1.5 to 2 m in height. 
The tunnel is ventilated by two ventilators from 
one side, and fresh air enters the tunnel from the 
front. A 4 mm diameter Teflon tube is used to sam­
ple the air, which is then injected into an infrared 
gas N2O and CH4 analyzer. The GHG are released 
into the fresh air as it exits the tunnel at the rear.

Eddy covariance method
The most widely used micrometeorological meth­
odology is Eddy covariance, which offers a direct 
measurement of vertical flux independent of air 
stability, works well for determining gas emissions 
from broad areas and does not rely on some of 
the oversimplifications assumed by other micro­
meteorological techniques [8]. This method has 
been used to measure the gas emissions from 
manure lagoons, soil N2O emissions, and rice pad­
dies CH4 emissions [49]. By tracking changes in 
gas concentration and the vertical movement 
of distinct air parcels, known as eddies, the 
eddy covariance (EC) approach quantifies gas 
emissions [12,50].

instantaneous vertical flux density over a surface 
with a radius of around 100 times the height [40].

Flux-gradient methods
By measuring the gas concentrations at two or 
more distinct heights and logging the horizontal 
wind speed, the flux-gradient method streamlines 
the observations required to calculate the vertical 
flux. Herein the vertical concentration gradient and 
turbulent diffusivity are combined to produce the 
vertical flux. The vertical concentration gradient is 
estimated using data from two different heights, 
and the turbulent diffusivity is calculated using 
wind speed and the turbulence readings. The 
measurements of the energy exchange at the sur­
face as well as the vertical concentrations of the 
gas, air temperature, and humidity can also be 
used to compute the vertical flux [42]. It is crucial 
to employ standard instruments for reading gas 
concentrations from various heights for applying 
flux-gradient procedures with high accuracy [8]. 
Although there are some distinctions, the flux- 
gradient hypothesis presupposes that molecular 
diffusion and turbulent gas transfer are the same 
process. While molecular diffusion originates from 
the random movement of molecules, turbulent 
transfer occurs when air parcels move from one 
level to another. The wind speed, height above 
the surface (such as soil, plant canopy, or water), 
vertical temperature gradient, and aerodynamic 
roughness of the surface all play a role in deter­
mining the actual magnitudes. Conventionally, if 
the concentration rises toward the surface, the 
gradient is positive; if not, it is negative. 
Measurements inside the constant flux layer of

By using satellite measurements, atmospheric

Discussion and recommendation
Major frameworks for measuring gas emissions, 
such as chamber-based, mass balance, micromete­
orological methodologies, isotopic techniques, 
Picarro gas analyzer method and satellite-based 
measurements have been reviewed along with 
related analytical and sensing methods. This article 
provides a comprehensive overview of the most 
suitable methods for GHG emission measurements, 
focusing on advancements in technology, instru­
mentations, ecological suitability and analytical 
techniques. The researcher’s requirements and 
land use, including the spatial/temporal variability, 
the availability of operators and equipment, the 
limits on the sampling sites, the targets or funding 
and maintenance expenditure mainly determine 
which methodology is the most useful one. 
Chamber methods (both closed and open) involve 
enclosing an area to measure gas concentrations 
changes over time. Chamber methods are widely 
used to estimate the gas emissions from agricul­
tural and animal feeding activities, though without 
a power supply, closed chambers can measure 
very low rates of GHG fluxes for pasture, cropland, 
rice paddy, wetland, drain, and ditches in a short 
period. Since they can be easily and inexpensively 
constructed from a variety of materials in a variety 
of styles, shapes, and sizes, closed chambers are 
best suited for some activities. The method allows 
for the measurement of GHG from various loca­
tions at different times using the same equipment 
and staff because the operation of the chambers 
and the evaluation are uncomplicated. Rising gas 
concentrations in the confined headspace cause 
nonlinear fluxes between the soil and the air, 
nevertheless, the air pressure within and outside 
the chamber can be regulated by using custom­
ized apertures. According to differences caused by 
chamber type, flow rates, and flow configurations, 
chambers are generally better suited for mitigation 
studies rather than choosing emission factors (data 
fusion, numerical modeling) for assessing GHG 
inventories. However, chamber methods are suit­
able for small-scale studies but may not represen­
tative for larger areas. Eddy covariance directly 
measures the exchange of gases in systems 
(between the atmosphere and soil), continuous 
data can be found but require sophisticated equip­
ment and expertise. The mass balance approach, 
dispersion modeling, flux gradient connections, or 
eddy covariance theory provide the basis for 
micrometeorological techniques. Most developing 
countries’ primary disadvantages with micro­
meteorological procedures are that they lack the 
expertise and understanding of these approaches, 
which are usually more costly and require specific 
analytical knowledge, expensive monitoring tools, 
and trained personnel for a successful application.

Micrometeorological approaches are useful for

monitoring remote areas but limited efficiency due

Conclusions
This review is purely methodological one which, 
on purpose, tried to familiarize the reader with the 
vast range of different developments and is nei­

review. Depending on the requirements and the 
gas of interest, each methodology has pros and 
cons. The inability to use analytical instruments for 
in-situ measurements due to their high cost and 
complexity necessitates the use of a laboratory to 
determine the gas concentrations in samples taken
